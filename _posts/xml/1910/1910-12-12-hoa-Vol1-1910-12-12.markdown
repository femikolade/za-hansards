<p>was cheered when he rose to propose: &#x201C;That the House go into Committee of Supply on the Estimates of Expenditure of the South African Railways and Harbours for the 10 months ending March 31, 1911.&#x201D; The mover said he noticed, when the Treasurer made his Budget speech the other day, that when his hon. friends came to the question whether there would be additional taxation or not, there was an eager, almost an anxious, attitude in certain quarters of that House. There was no such piquant element to deal with in what he (Mr. Sauer) had to say. The railways might almost be said to be offensive in their prosperity. The approximate capital expenditure&#x2014;that was, expenditure from borrowed money and money obtained from revenue <span class="col_695-696" refersTo="page_0357"/>which bad been brought into capital account for rolling-stock, was &#x00A3;13,664,000. <debateSection name="#reducing_railway_rates">

<heading>REDUCING RAILWAY RATES.</heading>

<p>With regard to the staff they had on the 31st of May, at the date of Union, 37,750 persons were employed on the railways; now they had 38,918, an increase of 1,168. Of these, 21,000 were Europeans. Besides the railways actually constructed&#x2014;7,045 miles&#x2014;there was actually under construction 000 miles more, at an approximate cost of about &#x00A3;4,000,000, or, to be exact, &#x00A3;4,052,084. Of these 900 miles, it was expected that 76 per cent., or 683 miles, would be completed within the next twelve months. Besides these, 810 miles, for which there was Parliamentary authority, at an estimated cost of &#x00A3;3,500,000, but in all cases the money was not available, and in some cases he was not sure that the lines would be to the best advantage, and in the public interest. Now he came to the revenue. It would seem, looking at the Estimates of the revenue up to the ten months ending 31st of March, that the revenue from traffic, leaving out subsidiary revenues, was &#x00A3;9,880,371. For the preceding ten months the revenue was &#x00A3;9,312,748. There was, therefore, an increase of &#x00A3;567,623. There were increases in all branches of traffic, but it was especially noticeable that there was &#x00A3;93,141 increase from passenger traffic. That, he said, was very noticeable, because in August of last year the C.S.A.R. reduced their passenger fares by something like 30 per cent. The Cape and Natal just prior to Union did the same thing also by something like 30 per cent., the estimated loss for these two colonies being &#x00A3;151,000. Notwithstanding that estimate, however, there was an increase in the passenger traffic, or an estimated increase of &#x00A3;98,000. That went to show that the depression to a very large extent had passed away, and it also showed that if they gave cheaper tickets, the public took advantage of them. He had said that the estimated revenue from traffic for the ten months to 31st of March was &#x00A3;9,880,371. That gave a monthly average, supposing they distributed that amount over ten months, of &#x00A3;986,037. Up to the end of November&#x2014;that was for six months &#x2014;they had actually received &#x00A3;6,061,785, or a monthly average, dividing the six by ten, of &#x00A3;1,010,298, whereas the monthly estimate was &#x00A3;988,037. The four months&#x2019; balance required to realise the estimate of &#x00A3;9,880,371 was &#x00A3;3,818,586, or dividing that sum again by the number of months still to run, an average per month of &#x00A3;954,646. That showed that the amount required less per month than the estimate was &#x00A3;33,391. Therefore, if they got the monthly estimate of &#x00A3;988,037, they would have a balance over revenue of &#x00A3;133,564. It was proposed to reduce the tariff, within the Cape Colony more especially, in fact, almost exclusively. That would entail a loss of &#x00A3;455,000 per annum. It was intended that the reduced rates should come into force in January. That meant that these rates would be in force for three months of the present financial period, the loss being &#x00A3;1(16,000. They were, however, only to come into force on the 1st of February next, and that meant one month less, amounting to &#x00A3;38,750. If they added that &#x00A3;38,750 to the &#x00A3;133,564 which they anticipated to get from the estimated revenue, they would get &#x00A3;172,314, which would very likely be in excess of the estimate. Now, he knew that prophecy was a gratuitous form of folly, but he could not help thinking that they would very likely, in fact, very probably, have a revenue of something like &#x00A3;200,000 in excess of the estimate. Now, with regard to the &#x00A3;465,000, he would like to say a few words. At the time of Union and today still, the railway rates were different in this Province from any other Province. Of course, he was talking of the local rates within the Province. In other words, they were higher. (Hear, hear.) Well, it seemed to him that one of the first objects of Union was to equalise local rates throughout the Union. Therefore, he discussed the matter with the gentlemen with whom he had the honour of being associated, and they decided that they should bring the Cape rates down to the same level as those in other parts of the Union. That reduction involved a loss of &#x00A3;465,000, and he would ask his friend for Cape Town (Mr. Jagger) particularly to notice, because he was such a particularist, that that fell to the advantage of the Cape Province almost exclusively. Natal got a very slight advantage, but the great bulk of the &#x00A3;465,000 was almost entirely to the advantage of the Cape Province. (Hear, hear.) They would be able to relieve Kimberley, Mafeking, Beaufort West, and various other centres from the grievances which they had suffered so long. He would beg leave to say that he entirely approved of and would do all he could, to carry out the provisions of the South Africa Act, which recognised that it would be necessary for the development of the country <span class="col_697-698" refersTo="page_0358"/>that they should carry goods and passengers up-country at the cheapest rate possible. The people living at the coast had contributed precious little towards the railways. They had never called out for higher railway rates, but they did not pay them themselves.</p>

<debateSection name="#working_expenditure">

<heading>WORKING EXPENDITURE.</heading>

<p>The ordinary working expenditure was &#x00A3;4,979,000. For the previous 10 months it was &#x00A3;4,632,000, and therefore there was an increase on the ordinary working expenditure of &#x00A3;347,000, or equal to 7.50 per cent. That was owing, firstly, to &#x00A3;269,000 on account of lines newly opened; secondly, to increased staff; and, thirdly, to increments to staff salaries&#x2014;a very considerable sum having had to be provided on account of emoluments of officers. To the amount of &#x00A3;4,979,000 must be added &#x00A3;791,000 for depreciation, making, therefore, a working expenditure, including depreciation, of &#x00A3;5,770,000. The provision made for the previous 10 months for depreciation was &#x00A3;382,000 only. There was, therefore, an increase of &#x00A3;408,000 in the item of depreciation, or an increase of 106 per cent. That was entirely owing to insufficient or no provision having been made previously in the Gape and Natal for depreciation. He thought every member of the House would agree that full provision should be made for depreciation, and he considered the amount now proposed was an adequate sum; but by no means an excessive sum. The expenditure with depreciation was &#x00A3;5,570,000, and the expenditure per train mile to March 31 next was estimated at 5s. 4d., as against an expenditure for the previous 10 months of 5s. 5.98d. per train mile, equal to a decrease of 1.57d., which was the lowest on record in South Africa. The percentage of earnings was not so favourable. The estimated cost per train mile far the 10 months ended March 31, including depreciation, was 58.40 per cent., as against the cost for the previous 10 months of 53.84 per cent.
