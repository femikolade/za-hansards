<from><span class="col_8121-8122" refersTo="page_0576"/>Mr <person refersTo="hansard_za">A E NOTHNAGEL</person>:</from>

<p>as Chairman, presented the Eighth Report of the Standing Select Committee on Home Affairs, dated 11 June 1986, as follows:</p>

<ol>

<li>1. Terms Of Reference</li>

</ol>

<p>The Standing Committee on Home Affairs having considered the subject of the Identification Bill [B 79&#x2014;86 (GA)], referred to it, your Committee begs to report the Bill with amendments [B 79a&#x2014;86 (GA)].</p>

<ol>

<li>2. Modus Operandi</li>

</ol>

<p>Oral evidence from officials of the Department of Home Affairs as well as the South African Police was heard. The Committee relied heavily on the comments of the Department of Home Affairs. The last-mentioned department consulted continuously with other Government Departments as well as with the private sector. The Committee also took cognisance of practices regarding identity documents in many other countries.</p>

<p>The Committee did not consider the principle as contained in the Population Registration Act, 1950, as the said Act was not under consideration.</p>

<p>Differences of opinion which arose in the Committee are reflected in the minutes of the Committee.</p>

<ol>

<li>3. Findings</li>

</ol>

<p>After thorough deliberation the Committee found as follows:</p>

<ol>

<li>3.1 The Committee was virtually unanimous in its opinion that a uniform identity document should be issued to all South African citizens. Identity documents to identify citizens of states are universally becoming ever more important and a more general practice.</li>

<li>3.2 The Committee took cognisance of the fact that the State has already had several million identity documents printed in order to replace the reference books, the so-called &#x201C;passbooks&#x201D;, of Black people with uniform identity documents.</li>

</ol>

<block name="quote">The Committee also noted that thousands of Black people have already made application for new identity documents, to replace the existing reference books.</block>

<ol>

<li>3.3 The Committee is of the opinion that an identity document is playing an increasingly essential role in the daily lives of the inhabitants of South Africa, not only in dealings with Government institutions, but also in dealings and transactions in the private sector.</li>

<li>3.4 With regard to the use of a race code the Committee, after much reflection, felt that the envisaged identity document for RSA citizens should not include a code number or any other reference to a person&#x2019;s race group in terms of the Population Registration Act or any other law.</li>

<li>3.5 The Committee expressed itself strongly in favour of the repeal of the Blacks (Abolition of Passes and Co-ordination of Documents) Act [Act No. 67 of 1952].</li>

</ol>

<block name="quote">The Committee is of the opinion that with this Bill the State is bringing into being identity documents for all South African citizens which contain no element of discrimination and which will be to the advantage of all South Africa&#x2019;s people.</block>

<ol>

<li>3.6 With regard to fingerprints the Committee was of the opinion that this is the most scientific method to prove a person&#x2019;s identity beyond all doubt. The Committee was therefore of the opinion that fingerprints should form part of a central identity register. The Committee also felt that it was correct that fingerprints should not appear in the identity document.</li>

</ol>

<block name="quote">With regard to fingerprints the Committee felt, however, that holders of existing identity documents, ie Whites, Coloureds and Indians, should have their fingerprints taken within a period of five years for recording <span class="col_8123-8124" refersTo="page_0577"/>in a central fingerprint register.</block>

<block name="quote">In terms of the Bill the fingerprints of all population groups must be taken when application is made for a new identity document or for the reissue of an existing identity document. In the case of existing documents, fingerprints will be phased in gradually.</block>

<ol>

<li>4. Recommendations>

<ol>

<li>4.1 <i>Amendments to Bill</i>

<ol>

<li>(a) The Committee recommends that clause 5 be amended to provide that a &#x201C;race code&#x201D; or other reference to race should not appear in the identity document.</li>

<li>(b) The Committee recommends that clause 6(3) be amended to provide that Whites, Coloureds and Indians must have their fingerprints taken within a period of five years for recording in a central fingerprint register.</li>

</ol></li>

<li>4.2 <i>General</i></li>

</ol></li>

</ol>

<block name="quote">The Committee was of the opinion that the State should urgently investigate more sophisticated methods of taking fingerprints than the present process.</block>

<block name="quote">The Committee also felt that the taking of fingerprints should where possible take place in the offices of the Department of Home Affairs where such offices are within reasonable reach. It was also felt that great discretion was necessary with the taking of fingerprints because, rightly or wrongly, certain misconceptions exist with regard to fingerprints.</block>

<p>Bill to be read a second time.</p>

</speech>

<p>Section 50 of the Internal Security Act, 1982 (Act 74 of 1982), was enacted on the recommendation of the Commission of Inquiry into Security Legislation as a measure with which to combat unrest. At the time of the commission&#x2019;s inquiry, unrest of the nature and extent to which it is presently being experienced, did not prevail. Notwithstanding the fact that section 28 of the Act also provides for preventive detention, the commission considered a provision such as section 50 necessary.</p>

<p>In the combating of unrest it is necessary for the South African Police to have powers of detention. Section 50 of the Act, which is still regarded as a necessary measure, is not effective in the combat of unrest of the nature and extent being experienced at present, as section 50 only provides for the detention of a person for a maximum period of 14 days. Experience has shown that the period of detention of 14 days which is provided for in section 50 is too short. Those who are involved in the unrest and who are detained in terms of the provision have to be released before the state of unrest in a specific area has been brought under control, with the result that their influence on the unrest cannot be neutralised.</p>

<p>It is essential to remove those concerned in fermenting unrest from the community for a long enough period of time in order to normalise the situation. There can be no doubt that the nature and extent of the unrest which has occurred since 1984 necessitate drastic measures. <p>*The provisions of the proposed section 50A will only be applied if the State President declares them to be applicable by proclamation in the <i>Gazette.</i> The State President may at any time suspend the operation of the section and thereafter, when circumstances necessitate it, declare it to be operative again.
