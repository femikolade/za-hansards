<p>Mr Chairman, when I participated in this debate on Friday, I raised several matters concerning agriculture, including the measures we have put into effect over the past few years to render assistance to farmers. My turn to speak came immediately after the hon the Minister of National Education had made a speech in which he dealt with certain political arguments that had been raised by hon members of the opposition parties. What was interesting of course was that when I raised facts regarding agricultural matters, particularly the allegation that the Government did not care very much for the farmers and did very little for them, hon members of the CP did not pay any attention to my exposition of what the Government had in fact done for the farmers. They were too interested in the political arguments of the hon the Minister of National Education. They sat in little groups over there, turned their backs on me in fact, and in that way simply pushed agriculture aside again. [Interjections.] Yes, they pushed agriculture aside again because politics is after all more important to them than the realities of agriculture, and they did this at a time when agriculture is experiencing serious problems.</p>

<p>You will find, Mr Chairman, that later today, after this discussion, we shall discuss agriculture again. Then politics and not the coherent facts will be at issue. Facts confuse the hon members of the CP. That is also why they do not want to work with new circumstances either. When I present them with the arguments, make the figures available in regard to how we have overcome difficult times, it is still said that not enough has been done, that more should still be done for the farmers. That refers to circumstances relating to the past. The history of the recent drought reveals one very interesting fact to us. It is that we find ourselves in new circumstances today. More aid to farmers and more schemes to help agriculture would only mean that we are trying to recreate the circumstances of the past. That, of course, is part of the solution. The further and biggest part of the solution is, however, to deal with the circumstances of today. There the hon members of the opposition parties have not given us any guidance at all. They have not told us what they regard as an alternative.</p>

<p>Of course, the same goes for their political policy. The greater the problems of the present are for them, the further they go back into the past to find a solution for them, while we today are dealing with realities that are future-oriented. This we really cannot change. We have to find solutions to these problems, and that is not a simple matter.</p>

<p>However, let me take it further. This view the CP has of agriculture aggravates the problems of agriculture because, to an increasing extent, we are having to decide what the future of agriculture is in the light of their political arguments. The more we politicise agriculture, the more difficult it is to solve the problems.</p>

<p>Let us identify the problems and the facts, let us work according to facts and let us utilise <span class="col_1447-1448" refersTo="page_0758"/>them. Then let us see if we can find a solution. Then we shall save agriculture. Then we shall be serving this country&#x2019;, because agriculture is experiencing difficult circumstances. I appeal once again to hon members of the opposition. Let us confine ourselves to the facts and let us also remove the sting from the discussion on agriculture that is going to follow, because the entire farming population is saddled with a problem that we shall have to solve together.</p>

<p>I want to touch on one more aspect; the question of own affairs and general affairs as it affects agriculture. The further one distances oneself from any industry or country, the more everything is merely general. However, things grow so complicated when one comes closer, that after a while one no longer knows where they belong. Therefore, the further one is removed from a problem, the more general it appears. The closer one comes to it, the more one sees that there are a variety of factors that have an effect.</p>

<p>This also applies to agriculture. It is very easy to say in agriculture: But agriculture is entirely an own affair. How far does one now have to go to draw the line? If one takes a normal farming activity such as a farm, one can say that the activities on that farm are centred entirely around Whites only. However, when one looks at what is produced on the land and at the product that has to be sold, one realises that it is not only the Whites who buy it. In the production process it is not only the Whites who are involved. There are also people of colour involved, members of other population groups. So whatever the circumstances may be, one can never say that one affair belongs entirely to one population group. Therefore, because agriculture makes use of many production sectors, it is important that we examine this. We should try to identify as closely as possible what belongs to one group. However, this can never be absolute, because we should always take into account this other component in the production process, regardless of what decision we want to take. This also applies to the marketing process, and that is why we shall always have to create freedom of movement for agriculture, particularly as far as general affairs are concerned.</p>

<p>Although 95<i>%</i> of agricultural activities, as the hon the Minister indicated, are basically conducted by the Whites group, we cannot regard it as 100<i>%</i> because marketing, labour and a variety of other factors also have to be taken into account. Therefore, as far as agriculture is concerned we have allowed most activities to fall mainly under own affairs, but there are important activities that fall under general affairs which one cannot deal with insensitively. To bring about this liason in general is just as much in the interests of agriculture as this major component of own affairs over which we have complete control.</p>

<p>We could debate this aspect again, but let us receive contributions from all sides of the House on how to improve and extend the concept of own affairs so that what is within our decision-making sphere, continues to remain there, but also so that others are given a fair say in what they share with us.</p>

<p>I gladly support this Budget because it is based on facts and has relevance to the realities.</p>

</speech>

<p>The report deals with a few important matters. Firstly it points out the importance of agriculture in the national economy. <p>Secondly, I want to say that agriculture supplies products, but it also uses products supplied by factories. <p>Furthermore, the report points out that agriculture is the second largest exporter in South Africa, and that in 1979 it was responsible for 20<i>%</i> of South Africa&#x2019;s exports. <p>It is also pointed out that agriculture has performed its primary function as a supplier of food and fibre in an excellent way: Production has grown faster than the population, so that each agriculturist fed 425 people in 1970 and this figure increased to 899 in 1985. <p>The report points out the importance of agriculture as far as decentralisation, the safeguarding of boarder areas, and so on are concerned. The conclusion arrived at here is that agriculture is of cardinal and fundamental importance to South Africa&#x2019;s economy.</p>

<p>The report goes on to show that the financial situation of farmers differs in the various regions.
