<p>I decided to speak on a subject today which, in my opinion, is in the interests of the country and is a subject with which I have been intimately involved for the past 15 years, viz housing. I want to exchange a few ideas specifically on the role, the purpose and the value of the utility housing corporations in South Africa.</p>

<p>At the Good Hope conference of business leaders which was held on 12 November 1981, the hon the State President, the Prime Minister at the time, announced that a decision had been taken that the then Department of Community Development would take the initiative for the establishment of utility housing corporations in the larger urban centres of our country. In my modest opinion this announcement was one of the greatest and most dynamic decisions ever taken in the search for solutions to the housing problems of our country.</p>

<p>The non-profitable utility housing corporation, or housing utility company as it is also called, makes it possible for the private sector, supported by the State, to make a true and positive contribution in the provision of housing in the long term, particularly for those in the lower and middle income categories. This housing concept is not at all strange to the Western World. Ebenezer Howard, the famous founder of the Garden Cities movement, propagated it as early as 1898. It is interesting too that in a country such as the Netherlands, 80% of all economic and low-cost housing is provided by housing corporations. There are more than 800 such movements in the Netherlands alone.</p>

<p>I cannot lay enough emphasis on the value and importance of this kind of housing corporation today. In all modesty I want to express the hope that the private sector will come forward more and more to make optimal use of this golden formula for the provision of housing. In passing the Housing Amendment Act No 63 of 1983, the State provided big incentives to make it possible and to make it easier for the private sector to become involved, and I want to praise and thank the Government for this today. Obviously the utility housing corporations will not be able to take over the total responsibility of housing, but even if one can succeed only in transferring 10% of the housing demand in this country to them, this in itself will remove a great burden from the State and relieve the housing pressure.</p>

<p>There is a group of people in every community&#x2014;they will always be there&#x2014;who are not the immediate responsibility of the Government, but cannot afford a house on the open market and cannot become the proud owners of a home. The utility housing corporations can provide a wonderful service for these people too. A housing principle which will never change is that one needs a house most when one&#x2019;s income is lowest. Unfortunately it is true that most people are caught up in this kind of situation at a young age as well as at an advanced age.</p>

<p>&#x2020;As a housing association is exempt from the payment of income tax and is obliged to use all surpluses for the furtherance of its objectives, the residents of its towns enjoy significant financial and social advantages. Apart from modest surpluses accruing from housing activities the housing association can generate extensive additional sources of income by undertaking large-scale developments including town centre complexes and the selling of land for the erection of cinemas, hotels, service stations, offices, shops etc all at market prices. Once a utility housing corporation has generated funds from these sources it can make those funds available to many clubs, societies, and educational and other social bodies in the community. It can even give further assistance in the form of donations of land, cash or interest free loans in order to build up a social environment in that specific community. However, a utility housing corporation can only be successful if it has a dedicated board of directors and a management who believe their task to be the implementing of housing as a social art and not a building or speculative undertaking.</p>

<p>*I exchanged a few ideas about the non-profitable utility housing corporation. It has been proven as a housing principle worldwide that if housing does not become a political football, but a vocation and a task, this <span class="col_565-566" refersTo="page_0311"/>kind of housing movement in particular can be successful. I am proud to be able to say today that South Africa has made wonderful progress in the establishment of housing utility companies. Greater progress can and must be made, and I trust that this sphere can be expanded with the continued encouragement and assistance of the State as well as the involvement of the private sector.</p>

<p>In his report on the appropriation and miscellaneous accounts in respect of the Administration: House of Assembly for 1984-85, the Auditor-General reported unauthorised expenditure totalling R854,36 with regard to Vote 2&#x2014;&#x201C;Agriculture and Water Supply&#x201D;. This amount was obtained irregularly during 1981-82 at an institute of that department by means of fictitious paysheets, for the furnishing of single quarters. The Treasury was not prepared to condone the irregular appropriation of Government money and accordingly reported the amount as unauthorised expenditure. The inclusion of this incident in the report of the Auditor-General was preceded by an investigation by the Directorate: Finance of the Department of Agriculture, in which the following became apparent:</p>

<block name="quote">Short courses were offered at the small grain centre of the Department of Agriculture and Water Supply at Bethlehem. Because the centre is situated far from the town the social club catered for meals for course-goers and the income collected was deposited in an entertainment account. As the funds of this entertainment account decreased rapidly the leader of the centre made a casual remark towards the accountant to the effect that attempts should be made to strengthen the funds. Without the knowledge of the leader of the centre the accountant, with the co-operation of the farm foreman, used a fictitious paysheet in respect of labourers on the farm. The amount obtained in this manner was kept separately and when the accountant was transferred he handed it over to the secretary of the leader of the centre. The accountant&#x2019;s successor reported the matter to the leader of the centre, and the management, after a lapse of time, decided erroneously to buy furniture for the single quarters which were recently completed. An amount of R816,32 was paid for the furniture and on the recommendation of the inspectors the balance of R38,04 was deposited in the revenue account. Although this procedure was unauthorised the State suffered no losses because the Department of Public Works and Land Affairs should in any event supply the furniture for the single quarters. The circumstances as outlined above were presented as evidence by the accounting officer of the Administration: House of Assembly during his appearance before the Standing Select Committee of the House of Assembly on Public Accounts.</block>

<p>The committee considered the evidence and in its report which was tabled on 20 July 1986, condemned the manner in which the funds had been obtained. Because the State had not suffered any loss, however, the committee recommended the sum of R854.36 for release by the House of Assembly. The consequence of the recommendation of the standing select committee is that the unauthorised expenditure of R854,36 must be authorised by the passing of special legislation by the House of Assembly. Such authorising legislation must be passed by the House of Assembly prior to 31 March 1987 to prevent the Auditor-General <span class="col_619-620" refersTo="page_0338"/>reporting it again in his report for the 1985-86 financial year. This Bill now before the House, will carry into effect the recommendation of the standing select committee that the expenditure be authorised.</p>
