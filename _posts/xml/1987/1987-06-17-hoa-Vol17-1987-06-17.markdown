<from>Mr <person refersTo="hansard_za">J J WALSH</person>:</from>

<p>Mr Speaker, prior to the adjournment of the House yesterday evening I was dealing with the subject of regional services council levies and the opposition thereto on the part of the chambers of commerce.</p>

<p>Assocom has appealed to the Government to reconsider the method of financing the RSCs by way of levies, since&#x2014;this is what they claim in their submission&#x2014;levies represent a grossly inefficient form of collecting finances and will have serious cost-raising consequences. Furthermore, they claim, the amount required has not been properly quantified and such levies will constitute a deterrent to employment. Finally, Assocom claims, RSCs will increase the overall tax burden on businesses.</p>

<p>I believe, Sir, that these are realistic and valid complaints, and consequently I support their plea. I also believe that the levies will have an impact on economic recovery and will dampen hopes of achieving the projected 3% real growth rate for this year. Furthermore one also questions why a levy should be placed on employment, therefore discouraging employment when job creation has been identified as a high priority. A levy on remuneration works precisely contrary to what we have to accomplish in this country viz fuller employment. One also asks why a complex structure has been introduced prior to an evaluation of the recommendations of the Margo Commission on Taxation. Costs of collecting the levies have been estimated at 80% of the total amount to be collected in the first year. One asks why the Government does not abandon the scheme and finance the RSCs from central Government revenues pending a decision on the Margo Commission Report.</p>

<p>Whilst on the subject of the Margo Commission Report, Mr Speaker, one also questions the procedure to be adopted in relation to the execution of its findings. We are told that the report is available but will only be released in August, after the Government has had the opportunity of studying it and of adopting overall guidelines. I believe this is contrary to good administration in that all parties should be allowed to comment prior to any decisions being taken. Interested parties are awaiting publication of the findings of the Margo Commission with bated breath. Such findings need to be evaluated from every quarter prior to the Government taking decisions which it might later regret.It is disappointing that the Government has decided not to follow this route.</p>

<p>Returning to RSCs, Mr Speaker, it has been argued that the levies are low and that they will therefore have little impact on business, that, Sir, despite the fact that businesses making a loss will still be liable for the levy. In addition, those people who can recall that GST commenced life in 1978 as a 4% tax will not be comforted at all by this argument. I believe that politically the racial structure of the regional services councils is unacceptable. Added to this, the apparent difficulty of their funding will merely confirm this non-viability.</p>

<p><span class="col_1637-1638" refersTo="page_0853"/>The other issue I wish to deal with this afternoon is the question of housing, and the distortion of property values resulting from the implementation of the Group Areas Act. On the question of housing the Standing Committee on Finance has noted with concern the housing shortage. Pending the publication of the President Council&#x2019;s report on the Group Areas Act, little is known as to what the Government&#x2019;s intentions are in this regard. Nevertheless, I believe that this Act has caused greater bitterness and interracial friction than probably any other in our country. This afternoon I wish to deal with the economic implications of the Act. That is that it has distorted property values and it is a deterrent to orderly property development and the creation of housing. It represents a millstone around the neck of those who have an earnest desire to resolve the housing crisis.</p>

<p>The first contention one hears among White conservatives is that without this Act, housing standards and values will drop. This is disputed as those who wish to reside in better areas will have the money to do so. Also they will have a vested interest in maintaining the standards that already exist. Trends in unofficial mixed areas have indicated a rise in value where areas have become mixed. There is ample evidence of this, particularly here in Cape Town. The South African Institute of Race Relations brief by Renfrew Christie, which was published in May of this year, was based on a survey of market attitudes in this connection. A persuasive majority of those consulted believe that property values will, in fact, go up in marginal areas should group areas restrictions be lifted and markets opened to all those who can afford to buy their own home.</p>

<p>More importantly though, Mr Speaker, repealing the Act will address the issues of overcrowding in our townships. The problem of low-cost housing will only be adequately addressed when the poor gain access to mass low-cost housing, the construction of which would generate income and enable them to enter the property market. The present racial zoning of land prevents sufficient new urban land from being released for this purpose. By the year 2000 it is agreed that there will be many more South Africans to be housed. The HSRC has estimated that the Black urban population will have increased from 6 million in 1985 to 19 million by the year 2000. This growth will largely occur in Durban, Cape Town and greater Johannesburg, and to house these people it is estimated that 1,5 million houses will have to be built between 1986 and 1990, with an additional half million in each subsequent five year period.</p>

<p>When compares that with what was achieved between 1981 and 1985, namely the completion of 41 000 houses for Blacks, one can see that we are very far short of that target. This presents an enormous challenge to the building industry. The activity of building houses will also create many new jobs which are obviously desperately needed in our country. However, the main problem and stumbling block to this development is the lack of available and suitable land.</p>

<p>In a paper on this subject published by Pauline Morris in August 1986 and entitled <i>Group Areas&#x2014;An Assessment and Change Implications</i>, the author states:</p>

<block name="quote">Many perceive the abolition of group areas legislation as meaning that Blacks, Indians and Coloureds will move into existing White areas. A far more important consequence is that sufficient land will become available and townships developed for all groups in response to natural socioeconomic forces. The abolition of group areas legislation and the allocation of land for Black settlement, instead of leading to swamping by other groups, will counteract this threat.</block>

<p>In summary the limits which the Group Areas Act places on the development of low-cost housing are far more important obstacles to property market growth than their oft-quoted effect on White areas. However, it is clearly not possible to deal with these subjects adequately this afternoon.</p>

<p>Returning to the Budget for 1987-88, I wish to stress that insufficient is being done in these two areas so vital to the future of our country, namely job creation and housing. These basic requirements for an educated, employed and adequately housed population are imperative for future prosperity free of instability and political unrest.</p>

<p>Measured in these terms this Budget fails to address these fundamental issues affecting our country. I therefore support the amendment <span class="col_1639-1640" refersTo="page_0854"/>moved by the hon member for Yeoville. [Interjections.]</p>

</speech>

<p>Mr Speaker, I listened with interest to the last portion of the hon member for Pinelands&#x2019; speech, and there is one point, in particular, on which I want to cross swords with him. Over the past 39 years this Government has done much more to provide housing for people of all groups&#x2014;White, Black, Coloured and Indian&#x2014;than has been done in the previous 300 years.</p>

<p>On another point I must admit that he is right. If we want stability in South Africa, I do believe with utter conviction that we must go out of our way to ensure that every person in South Africa owns his own home. I am prepared to admit that he is right, but that is a subject in itself.</p>

<p>This afternoon I should like to react to an argument the chief spokesman for the Official Opposition, the hon member for Barberton, and the hon member for Yeoville advanced yesterday. It involved the increase in expenditure on the TBVC countries, in particular the guaranteeing of loans which are negotiated by those countries and which, according to the hon members, are in fact not reflected in the South African budget. The hon member for Yeoville, in particular, specifically quoted from the evidence furnished on this specific subject to the Standing Committee on Finance by the hon the Minister of Foreign Affairs. I do want to say that the hon member&#x2014;he is not here yet&#x2014;quoted very selectively. The hon the Minister mentioned many other positive aspects, but of course the hon member conveniently omitted to mention them.</p>

<p>For me, however, it is not so much what the hon members for Yeoville and Barberton said; for me it is the irony of who it is who said it. On the one hand it came from the chief spokesman of the Official Opposition whose policy is one of total partition and for whom the costs involved in making an independent state a viable entity must have been a revelation. One cannot simply cut a people off, tell it that there, it has its independence, and then leave it its fate. It simply cannot work. They have always been a part of us and we have no option but to care for them. One can never force independence on people. One cannot force partition on people; it must be worth their while; there must be something in it for them. They must ask for it.</p>

<p>I have often asked myself on what grounds one grants people independence. It is clear to me that the areas involved must be areas in which the people have traditionally lived. They must be a homogeneous group and have their own language, culture and history. The most important requirement, however, is that they should ask for it. The TBVC states requested it; the others did not. The fact is that it was at a very low level that they had to base their start. As far as I am concerned, that is the gist of the matter. I want to tell hon members today that we have quite simply not helped them enough. Their problem of not being recognised by the world has led to their not being able to obtain help from elsewhere.</p>

<p>Let us take the situation of Lesotho. It obtained its independence from England in precisely the same way that the TBVC countries obtained their independence from South Africa. Lesotho, however, had international recognition. Lesotho can vote in the UN. It can borrow money from the World Bank and the IMF. The weirder its conduct towards South Africa, the more assistance it obtains. The end result of that has been that Lesotho and some of the other self-governing and independent states around us have become beggars.</p>

<p>Someone else who was not happy with the situation was the hon member for Yeoville. I am terribly sorry he is not here, because I enjoy speaking to him. He is the great self-acknowledged social democrat whose noble goal is to give everyone in the country a fair chance. That is a wonderful goal. I am in complete agreement with him about that.</p>

<p>The answer is merely that without the additional assistance, in the form of guarantees, the people in the TBVC countries are simply not being given a fair chance. That is how simple it is.</p>

<p>He accused the hon the Minister of the fact that the budget had been circumvented by way of those guarantees. The reason why the issuing of guarantees is not reflected in the national budget is that a guarantee form is only a potential liability as far as the <span class="col_1641-1642" refersTo="page_0855"/>Exchequer is concerned. After all, it is not yet a tangible demand on State funds. If a borrower, supported by an RSA guarantee, were to neglect to meet his obligations, the guaranteed liabilities would be budgeted for in terms of normal procedures.</p>

<p>The hon member for Yeoville has served on the Standing Committee on Public Accounts for years now and is still a serving member. Both of us have been members for a long time. In the Auditor-General&#x2019;s annual report to that committee all guarantees are set out fully each year. He will see the amount of R778 million in the Auditor-General&#x2019;s report for the 1986-87 financial year.</p>

<p>The point, however, is that there is now a &#x201C;Hobson&#x2019;s choice&#x201D;. The South African taxpayer could simply not afford it any longer as far as the TBVC countries were concerned. The TBVC countries nevertheless have greater needs which simply must be accommodated. That is why the Development Bank of Southern Africa is investigating the situation and devising requisite plans including, amongst other things&#x2014;I shall not be going into this in detail&#x2014;a &#x201C;Joint Presidential Committee&#x201D; in Ciskei and financial adjustment committees for the other three countries, the establishment of a five-year plan to start paying interest on capital and approval for the monitoring of expenditure, something I shall be coming back to.</p>

<p>What is interesting is that when Britain granted independence to Lesotho, they continued to keep a watch on the Exchequer. We have gone much further. We have granted the TBVC countries full independence. It is at their insistence that we are now helping to place people in a position to supervise their own Exchequer.</p>

<p>There is also the erroneous impression that the relevant states make no contribution, or virtually no contribution, to the Exchequer. It is completely wrong and unfair to those countries to imply any such thing. By way of income tax, GST, customs and exise, company tax, etc, the individual contributions of these states come to more than 50% of their total annual expenditure. If examples of misspending do occur, which in effect means unnecessary, ill-considered expenditure, such as that to which the hon members for Barberton and Yeoville referred in the standing committee, in particular, half of it is their own money. It is also calculated that the citizens of these four states spend up to R1 billion in GST in South Africa.</p>

<p>South Africa&#x2019;s Minister of Finance is, in conjunction with the Ministers of Finance of the TBVC countries, giving serious attention to the harmonising of taxation. I am mentioning these aspects to prove to hon members that serious attention is being given to the financial situation in these countries.</p>

<p>There is also something else I should very much like to mention to the hon member. With independence for the TBVC countries, South Africa effectively transferred additional obligations concerning, and responsibility for, six million people to these young, inexperienced governments. It can safely be said that today South Africa would have had to shoulder a much greater financial burden if these people had still been part of South Africa. There would have been millions of additional people streaming into the cities had it not have been for the policy of decentralisation and the creation of job-opportunities and industries in those areas.</p>

<p>What is more, initially it was mistakenly accepted that the revenue bases of these states would at least develop at the same rate as the increase in the expected levels of expenditure. That is unfortunately not the case; new countries will have much greater needs. The result is that the gap between revenue and expenditure simply increased. After a period of ten years in Transkei, and after a five-year period in Ciskei, and so on, I think I can say that our initial calculations of the &#x201C;school fees&#x201D; these countries would have to pay, and still have to pay, were in fact inadequate. The self-administration of a country is a tremendous responsibility, and we cannot ever allow those people to be left to their fate, to be thrown to the dogs.</p>

<p>That the independent states would, in time, encounter financial problems was therefore unavoidable. There is no question about that. They could borrow against their own assets, and up to 1 April 1986 they borrowed R2,5 billion on their own initiative. Provision is being made for that money&#x2014;interest and repayments&#x2014;in their latest budgets. It has also become necessary, however, for us to help them shoulder their wagons up the <span class="col_1643-1644" refersTo="page_0856"/>incline. During 1986-87 the alternative sources of borrowing for those people were quite simply depleted, and the governments of the TBVC countries were compelled, one after the other, to turn to South Africa and appeal to us for help in an endeavour to maintain their services and the stability they offer.</p>

<p>I have said that loans to the value of R778 million were guaranteed last year. This year the amount is R620 million. The hon member for Yeoville asked how the states arrived at the amounts in their budgets. He wanted to know whether they were simply random amounts laid down by them, or whether South Africa simply had to guarantee the rest if they themselves could not afford to do so.</p>

<p>As part of this five-year plan certain specific guidelines were laid down. I should like to mention a few. The percentage increase in the individual revenues of those countries, excluding loans, must be higher, for example, than the percentage increase in the expenditure plus capital redemption. Secondly, individual sources of revenue, excluding revenue from customs and excise, must be utilised and developed in such a manner that there is, at the very least, an average growth rate of 20% per annum. Thirdly, current expenditure must be restricted to within the limits of a 10% per annum growth rate. So very stringent conditions are now being imposed on these people. A fourth condition is that capital investment must constitute 25% of their total investments.</p>

<p>We made a promise, and that was that the assistance that South Africa would grant these countries would increase by 25% per annum. If we do this we should, within five years, achieve a position of equilibrium and they will no longer have to borrow any money from outside sources.</p>

<p>Against that background let me tell these two hon gentleman that it ought to be clear that the financial problems of the TBVC countries definitely cannot be solved in one year. The situation has, however, very specifically been focused upon with a view to bringing it under control within five years. In the present-day situation it is a question of stability for the sake of maintaining services. If we wish to achieve a situation of equality in those countries, we would need, not R2 billion but R3,2 billion. That is the cost involved.</p>

<p>I have said that we have a choice, a &#x201C;Hobson&#x2019;s choice&#x201D;, between what South Africa can do and what these countries must receive. I want to add that if we did not grant this assistance, the results would be catastrophic, not only for those countries themselves, but also for stability in Southern Africa.</p>

<p>My task in the Department of Foreign Affairs specifically relates to Africa and, even more specifically, to these TBVC countries. In assessing these countries one must look with great realism at South Africa&#x2019;s position. I have no doubt that South Africa has reached the crossroads. One therefore has to take note of the realities of the country, not shy away from them, not stick one&#x2019;s head into the sand, as so many people are so prone to do. We shall have to solve South Africa&#x2019;s problems in a positive manner, with realistic idealism.</p>

<p>The question is whether we can adapt. Can we adapt to present-day realities? There are many days, as I look around me, on which I doubt whether all the hon members can adapt to the realities. South Africa is not only the fatherland of five million White people; South Africa is the fatherland of 30 million people. South Africa&#x2019;s people, White, Black and Coloured, need one another in this country. South Africa&#x2019;s economy is completely interdependent. The best way of looking after the White man in this country is to look after the Black man. South Africa is a First-World and a Third-World country. It is a developed and a developing country, and the great test in regard to this problem, when it comes to the TBVC countries, is what would happen if we simply turned off the tap.</p>

<p>We are not going to allow the same thing to happen in South Africa that has happened in the rest of Africa. We are simply not going to allow overall standards to decline and South Africa to become a Third-World country. We are not going to allow South Africa to degenerate into chaos and go up in flames, as has so frequently happened in Africa. Our task in Africa and in Southern Africa is specifically to uplift people, nations and peoples, to increase their standards. That is what it is all about.</p>

<p><span class="col_1645-1646" refersTo="page_0857"/>I wonder how many hon members who are so critical of the TBVC countries have even taken the trouble to look at what goes on there. I should like to extend the promise today that even before the end of the year I will arrange for hon members to have an opportunity to visit the TBVC countries. These countries form the Third-World component of South Africa; they are the deprived areas of South Africa.</p>

<p>The CP says we must give everyone independence, implement partition and simply let things slide. [Interjections.] The PFP again says we should proceed as in the past. The result is that no one would then invest there and no job opportunities would be created. No new infrastructure would be created there and the difference between the First-World and Third-World components in Southern Africa would simply grow larger.</p>

<p>South Africa&#x2019;s greatest danger is not communism, Russian imperialism and so on, but rather selfishness and self-righteousness. At least the Government cannot be accused of that. One need only look at the infrastructure created in the TBVC countries, and at the schools, hospitals and dams which have been built there. One needs to have a look at the industrial and agricultural development that has taken place there. One needs to look at who is involved in those projects. [Interjections.]</p>

<p>The other day I was very impressed by one of the numerous factories in Venda. There two Taiwanese had started a jersy manufacturing factory. The factory employs 450 Venda women, people who previously had no jobs.</p>
