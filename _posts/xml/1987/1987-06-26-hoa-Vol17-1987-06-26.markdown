<speech by="#minister_of_environment_affairs">

<from>The <person refersTo="hansard_za">MINISTER OF ENVIRONMENT AFFAIRS</person>:</from>

<p>Mr Chairman, I move:</p>

<block name="quote">That the Bill be now read a second time.</block>

<p><span class="col_2329-2330" refersTo="page_1199"/>The Fisheries Development Corporation of South Africa, Limited, generally known as Viskor, was established by the Fishing Industry Development Act, Act No 44 of 1944. This Act was repealed by the Fishing Industry Development Act, Act No 86 of 1978, in terms of which Viskor continued to exist as a juristic person.</p>

<p>The powers of Viskor were essentially of a dual nature, namely to make loans available at subeconomic rates of interest for the development of community services and for the purchase of fishing boats and equipment, and to take up shares in fishing companies and share in the profits of such companies in order to compensate for the low rates of interest.</p>

<p>Apart from promoting the fishing industry by taking up shares in developing fishing companies and the financing of fishing boats and equipment, Viskor also played a significant role in the social field by way of the establishment and management of housing, electricity and water schemes as well as other facilities for fishermen. In time these assets were, however, taken over by fishing companies and local authorities with the assistance of loans at a low rate of interest. By virtue of the Government&#x2019;s accepted privatisation policy, and on account of a function-centred investigation into the rationalisation of Viskor by the Commission for Administration, it was decided that the corporation should be liquidated and that its financing powers with regard to the promotion and development of the fishing industry should be transferred to the Small Business Development Corporation, and its technical and research functions to the State.</p>

<p>In terms of section 31 of the Fishing Industry Development Act, 1978, the corporation can, however, only be liquidated on the authority of an Act of Parliament. The object of the Bill before the House is to give effect to this. In order to accomplish the efficient and meaningful liquidation of the corporation, clauses 3 and 9 of the Bill regulate the passing of the assets, liabilities, rights and obligations of Viskor as agreed upon to the Small Business Development Corporation and the State. Clause 3 of the Bill makes further provision for the continued existence of the board of directors of Viskor to enable the board to finalise the affairs of the board, whereupon it will cease to exist.</p>

<p>In order to ensure the utilisation, to the benefit of the fishing industry, of money paid over to the Small Business Development Corporation by Viskor, as well as revenue obtained in respect of the repayment of loan debts and any portion of the investment portfolio, clause 6 of the Bill makes provision for such money and revenue to be deposited, in a special fund for this purpose, by the Small Business Development Corporation.</p>

The advisory council which is being established, advises the hon the Minister of Transport Affairs and the two departments that fall under him on policy and policy aspects. That is why it is essential in my opinion that the hon the Minister of Transport Affairs appoint the chairman and vice-chairman he wanted to advise him and to serve on the council.</p>

<p>I come back to the composition of 38 members proposed in the legislation by the Department of Transport Affairs. The standing committee thought fit, after reaching consensus, to extend this to 40 members. I think it is essential that since the function of this committee is mainly an advisory one, it should be as representative as possible. This committee has to cover a large field and serve a lot of interest groups. In the second place, it follows that the majority&#x2014;approximately 23 to 25&#x2014;of these members will come from the private sector. I think the principle stated as an objective by all the memoranda and evidence that was submitted, viz that the government sector be in the complete minority as against the private sector which is by far in the majority, was complied with.</p>

<p>In studying the recommendations of the National Transport Policy Study, the clear conclusion was reached that the hon the Minister would be placed in an unenviable position in that he would have to nominate the appropriate organisations whereas only 15 could be nominated. The hon the Minister will then also have to appoint the people and receive recommendations about this from the organisations. I think the new way is much better. The legislation deviates from the National Tansport Policy Study, which now has 40 members and in which the organisations have been nominated. The organisations will recommend people, and the hon the Minister of Transport Affairs will make up his transport advisory council from these recommendations, so that we can all co-operate in promoting the interests of transport.</p>

<p>In my opinion we are obviating two important problems with those new compositions. In the first place the hon the Minister is no longer being placed in the impossible position of having to choose whom he wants to appoint. That function is being transferred to the respective organisations which will nominate people.</p>

<p>Secondly I am convinced that this will force the organisations to interpret their role in this advisory council in the correct way. I do not think that anyone who maintains in future that he had no say in the matter, can blame the Government or the hon the Minister of Transport Affairs for that. In that case we should have to go back and see which organisation was involved and which people they had nominated in that organisation. I think the new tendency that is being consolidated in the transport advisory council, and in which we have moved away from the recommendations of the National Transport Policy Study, completely obviates this problem which elicited a lot of criticism in the past.</p>

<p>While I am on the subject, I also want to say that although there are a number of organisations which are going to disappear when the transport advisory council is established, and there are organisations which are going to disappear in the medium term, such as the National Transport Commission and the road transportation boards, I should like to convey our sincere thanks at this early stage to all the people who served in these organisations over many years. I think those people played their part and made their contribution to developing the transport sector, which contributes approximately 9% of the gross national product today, and we on this side of the House want to convey our sincere thanks to them.</p>

<p>As is the case with everything in life, we have reached the stage at which renewal is necessary in this country, not only on the constitutional <span class="col_2377-2378" refersTo="page_1223"/>level, but also in various associated spheres of which transport is one. This aspect is part and parcel of that necessity, as is the whole aspect of a greater say by the private sector as well as the devolution of power.</p>

<p>That is why we feel it is a good thing to effect a change in transport affairs at this stage by establishing the transport advisory council. As I have said, the transport advisory council is the initial stage of the new transport policy.</p>

<p>We have a further problem, however, and I should like to ask the hon the Deputy Minister something. I would appreciate it if the hon the Deputy Minister could tell us in his reply when we shall reach the second and third stages at which we shall really begin to implement the whole new transport policy, because there are a number of new measures resulting from the National Transport Policy Study, that have to be passed.</p>

<p>The most important questions in this connection concern what will happen to the South African Transport Services; what will happen to the permit system; what will happen to the subsidy system; and, most important, what will happen to the cross-subsidisation. In adding all those matters together, one important aspect remains. Hopefully the hon the Minister will give us an insight into it in his reply.</p>

<p>On which date do the hon the Minister and his department envisage the possibility that the new laws of the National Transport Policy Study will be implemented, and how long does the hon the Minister envisage it will take to solve the above-mentioned problems by implementing the new South African transport policy?</p>

<p>I find it very interesting that the South African transport sector was very enthusiastic in the beginning. Lately we have been getting the impression in the standing committee, however, that some of these bodies that were so enthusiastic before, are back-pedalling. It is interesting that privatisation and deregulation will not require sacrifices from the Government only.</p>

<p>We must realise that deregulation has advantages and disadvantages for everyone in the transport sector. It is going to be detrimental to some of those that belong to the Government, but it will also have certain advantages. At the same time, however, it will have certain advantages and disadvantages for the private sector.</p>

<p>We are getting a certain impression from those people who, together, are ultimately going to take the decisions on what the transport policy of South Africa will involve. There are people who were initially enthusiastic, but reversed their position because they were dissatisfied, and began to lay down conditions for the implementation of the new transport policy.</p>

<p>I want to make a request to the hon the Deputy Minister that we adhere more strictly to the rules that were spelt out in the National Transport Policy Study and in the White Paper on transport. I do not think we should start back-pedalling and postponing things year after year. The former Minister of Transport Affairs, Minister Hendrik Schoeman, made certain promises which were to have been realised over a period of time. I think that hon Minister was correct when he made those promises. In the meantime the period he envisaged has expired, however, and there are a number of questions from the public about when we shall reach the stage the Minister envisaged. I think it is desirable to reach that stage as soon as possible and to deal with it in a responsible way.</p>

<p>In conclusion I want to deal with certain aspects of the legislation itself. Basically the Bill consists of two very important clauses, apart from the explanation about the members. I have said that the members come mainly from the private sector, which I think is a good thing because these are the people who want to have a say with the hon the Minister. I want to say something to the appointed transport advisory council, and that is that the emphasis must be on the word &#x201C;advice&#x201D;. Ultimately policy will still be made by the hon the Minister, the Cabinet and the <span class="col_2379-2380" refersTo="page_1224"/>department. I think we must make this matter very clear at a very early stage to everyone who is going to participate in this discussion about the composition and the function of this council. The emphasis of this council is on advice and the Government of the day will continue to prescribe policy.</p>

<p>As far as the composition of that council is concerned, the standing committee decided to appoint a management committee so that decisions could be taken much more quickly than was anticipated earlier in the Act. The important point is to ensure that if the transport advisory council advises the hon the Minister, the hon the Deputy Minister and the department, this advice is implemented as soon as possible. It is important that problem areas be eliminated as soon as possible. That is why a certain change was made by clause 7, specifically providing for the accommodation of that acceleration of recommendations.</p>

<p>Another very important clause is the one that provides for the appointment of subcommittees. I am convinced that this advisory council will work, because provision is being made for subcommittees which consist not only of members, but also of experts. When these subcommittees sit, it is possible under certain conditions for them to co-opt experts to help them to accelerate and facilitate the process, and to advise the Government as soon as possible so that the Government can take action.</p>

<p>One further important point follows, viz that these people do not have the right to vote. Any possible criticism, therefore, that this is a way of accommodating more people than the legislation provides for, does not hold water. There are 40 members who have the right to vote and those 40 members ensure that the legislation will be applied under all conditions. There is a possibility, however, that if there are not enough experts among those 40 members, they can be replaced by other experts who can make a contribution to assist us with the problem in the short term.</p>

No committee can function without information.
