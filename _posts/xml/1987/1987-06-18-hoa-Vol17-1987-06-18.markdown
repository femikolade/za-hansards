<p>Mr Chairman, one subject which has come up time and again over the past few days&#x2014;in fact since the beginning of the session&#x2014;has been the issue of partition. The purport of remarks made about this matter has varied; sometimes it has been very derogatory, particularly when reference was being made to hon members on this side of the House. When one hears and reads the statements made by people on the NP side, however, it is very clear that there is a dissension in the statements of hon members on the Government side. In the first place we find among those hon members those who regard it as an ideal model. I do not believe there is anyone who would find fault when we say that it is indeed an ideal model. The question is whether one wants to pursue the ideal or whether one simply wishes to acknowledge it in passing, say it is not feasible and then simply forget about it.</p>

<p>In the second place there are those who say that it is not the whole solution. By that is meant that it may be part of the solution, but that another part of the solution to the question of regulating relationships among the various peoples in this Southernland of ours is to be found in a different sphere than the dividing up of territory.</p>

<p>Then, thirdly, there are those who say without further ado that partition is outdated. They add that it is simply ridiculous. We encounter that approach, too, in this House; the approach that it is ridiculous and outdated and that we would do better to forget about it. These people contend that we should now move in the direction of a unitary state. Within that unitary state there will, it is true, be an occasional subdivision of territory, but there will be only one government with a joint decision-making process up to the highest level in one undivided South Africa.</p>

<p>Then there are those who adopt a somewhat more moderate approach and say that it was <span class="col_1757-1758" refersTo="page_0913"/>a good thing up to a certain stage but that in fact we persisted with it for five years too long. Here I shall just quote the odd sentence from a propaganda document distributed by the NP in the Eastern Cape. It is only a photostated document. Here reference is made to the right-wing groups of the CP and the HNP &#x201C;with their reverse-gear policy&#x201D;. They go on to say:</p>

<block name="quote">Terug na partisie. Nog verder terug as afsonderlike ontwikkeling (wat die Nasionale Party reeds beproef het en waarvan die moontlikheid nou uitgeput is) trouens die Nasionale Party het miskien vyf of meer jaar te lank daarmee aangehou.</block>

<p>I think it is fair to ask a few questions and make a few statements in this regard.</p>

<p>The first is: What has the NP done in this connection? The NP cannot make out that it does not want to accept responsibility for what it has done for decades in this regard. The NP, and not only the NP but also other governments before it, are responsible for the fact that certain territories were set aside and demarcated under their own administrations for the Coloureds, with the effect that 100 000 hectares became Coloured group areas. In addition there is 2 million morgen&#x2014;probably that would be approximately 1,7 million ha&#x2014; in the rural areas that is the property of the Coloureds. It was set aside for them. It is under their management, and the NP supports increasingly higher forms of management for those territories.</p>

<p>That is partition. It is a form of partition. I do not think anyone can dispute that; it is so. I do not believe that that side of the House is prepared to go back on these steps they have taken, annul the delimitation of those areas and simply leave the Coloureds at the mercy of the whole of South Africa again, in a situation where each of them individually would have to fight for a piece of land.</p>

<p>Moreover, it is true that under NP policy four Black peoples have become independent, with their own territory as a basis. That is partition. That is the delimitation of territory, and on that territory is a specific people whose territory it is. Of course, I find it regrettable&#x2014;I do not wish to digress too far&#x2014; that the NP consented to the loss of the name of a specific people linked to a specific national state, and consented only to talk about certain territories. It is true that in the territories as they have been delimited, specific ethnic groups have been established, but I do think that this is a movement away from the course that the NP has followed thus far, namely to establish a specific territory for a specific people.</p>

<p>Moreover it is true that six Black peoples have accepted self-government. The self-government was a political function or power that they did not exercise in a vacuum. They exercised that political self-determination within a specific territory, and that is partition. It is apartheid. What I mean to say with these words is that I am not one of those who approves of everything done in the name of apartheid, but on the other hand I do not condemn apartheid either, because in terms of the policy of separate development or apartheid, as some people see it, much has been done in South Africa and much of it was good, so much so that last year the president of a Black people said that it was a good policy. He praised the NP for it, because it had brought freedom to him and his people.</p>

<p>There is another statement I want to make. The NP still respects its own work. Those who say that it is ridiculous; that it is past; that it has no further meaning, or whatever, forget that the NP today still respects that work that it has done. Surely it still respects the existence of four independent peoples within their own territories. Surely it still recognises and respects the existence of six self-governing states within their own territories. It has always respected partition. Surely there is no doubt on that score. We are not squabbling about that. After all, it will not take the group areas, the 100 000 hectares, away from the Coloured people. Surely it would prefer, like this party, to enlarge those territories meaningfully, to make a more meaningful Coloured community life possible. Therefore, within the structure and system of partition it will think as we do and move in that direction.</p>

<p>I shall make a third statement, and it is that if it is being suggested that the NP persisted with this policy five years too long, then that is in conflict with practice. It is in conflict with what the NP itself did and has done up to now.</p>

<p><span class="col_1759-1760" refersTo="page_0914"/>What has the NP, and the Government that preceded it, paid for the purchases and consolidation of land for the homelands over fifty years? I ascertained the amount from the department, and it is R1 219 418 000. In terms of the liberation of a people and the fact that one has therewith not only created for specific peoples their own geographic basis for political expression, but also paid a price for a geographic basis for White self-determination, then that is a small price to pay for it.</p>

<p>That price and what we have achieved with it comprised part of partition, and it did not stop five years ago when it began to be said that separate development and national states were not the entire policy. On 31 December 1986 that was the amount that had been spent in the course of 50 years.</p>

<p>That is part of the price for the self-determination of Black peoples and orderly coexistence, and not friction-creating intermingling. It also is part of the price of White self-determination. The process has of necessity not run its course; naturally it may continue. If that side of the House gives us an indication that they are prepared to think further along those lines&#x2014;a great deal of thinking will still be necessary&#x2014;they may be assured of the unambiguous support of this side of the House in that regard when it comes to the safeguarding of White self-determination in their own territory.</p>

<p>Surely partition is not so alien to the NP&#x2019;s present-day practice. I am not arguing against the hon the Minister of Finance, but let me quote from this year&#x2019;s Budget for partitioned Black states. I hope I am quoting correctly, since I am not entirely <i>au fait</i> with financial matters, but I think I am quoting a few very simple figures correctly. This Budget reflects an amount of R1,372 billion in respect of independent states, apart from the R620 million guarantee for loans. I think I am correct in putting it that way. The amount of R2,488 billion is budgeted for development aid to six national states. That is more than double what we paid over 50 years in respect of land purchases for the consolidation of Black states. Under Foreign Affairs R1,875 billion is being budgeted for transfer payments.</p>

<p>As far as the Department of Development Planning is concerned, the transfer payments amount to R843,563 million. The total amount, which only includes a few amounts&#x2014; this is my own arithmetic; I hope it is correct&#x2014;is R7,199 billion. This is what the Government spent on the basis of recognition of partitioned Black states. Therefore it does not make sense for hon members on that side of the House to speak about partition as outdated and to say that there is no longer any sense in it.</p>

<p>I want to go a little further. The NP also promises independence. It is not only that it gives self-governing states this assistance; it also promises them independence. Here the NP has the full support of this side of the House; we are not in dispute with one another on that score. We just want that side of the House to stop writing off partition, as a principle and an ideal, as ridiculous.</p>

<p>Partition is still so important to the NP that less than two years ago the hon the State President said in Bloemfontein:</p>

<block name="quote">I wish to state emphatically that the sovereignty of these states is not in dispute whatsoever.</block>

<p>We are in accord with that. The sovereignty of these states is certainly not in dispute. It is therefore maintaining what it has brought into being in practice in accordance with the principle of partition.</p>

<p>However, there are a few statements in this regard that create problems for me. Within the structure of partition the question of citizenship and political expression is relevant and we on this side of the House of course have problems in this regard. After the hon the State President said, &#x201C;I wish to state emphatically that the sovereignty of these states is not in dispute whatsoever&#x201D;, he was reported as follows:</p>

<block name="quote">As he had already mentioned the leaders of these countries had in various discussions also raised requests of citizenship with regard to their own inhabitants.</block>

<p>Then he said:</p>

<block name="quote">I am prepared to negotiate this with these independent countries.</block>

<p>Hon members must note that this is not negotiation in regard to the members of a <span class="col_1761-1762" refersTo="page_0915"/>specific ethnic group for whom a homeland, a national state, has been created, although they reside outside that state; no, it is the &#x201C;inhabitants of these countries&#x201D;, in other words the citizens of Bophuthatswana living within Bophuthatswana, Xhosas living inside Transkei and Ciskei and Vendas residing in Venda. I quote further:</p>

<block name="quote">Thus the South African Government is prepared to negotiate with these four governments about restoring the South African citizenship of members of the Black communities residing within&#x2026;</block>

<p>within&#x2014;</p>

<block name="quote">&#x2026;the borders of Transkei, Bophuthatswana, Venda and Ciskei or who lost that citizenship as a result of independence.</block>

<p>We have problems in that regard. We do not have any difficulties about the fact that the NP has come a long way in the implementation of partition, but we do have problems with the introduction, at this juncture, of this dual citizenship. There is citizenship of four states that the NP has made independent, and when South African citizenship is again granted to them we say that we have problems in that regard. The issue here is the granting of South African citizenship to citizens of independent states.</p>

<p>The CP wishes to ask a few questions in this regard. The first question concerns the political content of this dual citizenship. Does citizenship, in the case of an independent state, mean that the citizen living there exercises full political rights there, namely the right to vote and to possess land there and, under the term &#x201C;citizenship&#x201D;, enjoy all the rights of an ordinary citizen, but when it comes to South Africa it means something else? What other meaning does it have?</p>

<p>My second question is whether this is merely concerned with travel documents. I had the privilege of serving in the Cabinet. I participated in discussion groups in which we discussed citizenship and the need of the citizens of the Black states to travel abroad. We then discussed whether we could accommodate those people in some way by way of travel documents, etc.</p>

<p>Now I ask again: Is this what the hon the State President envisages here? Is it purely a matter of travel documents? What is the political significance of the concept of &#x201C;citizenship&#x201D; that it also wishes to grant to the inhabitants of the independent Black states? To me, citizenship implies political rights, the franchise, the right, if the people want me, to sit in this House, and at present I enjoy that privilege. However that is not what it could mean to a Black man from Venda or Transkei. If it cannot mean that to him, then what we have is a confusion of concepts. Then what we have is dissension in regard to the content of a specific and very important political concept.</p>

<p>Then there is a further question: If the current meaning or content of the political concept of citizenship is that one may exercise political rights, vote and even take one&#x2019;s place as a representative in the highest debating chamber of that country as the representative of voters, then surely it goes without saying that if that is the meaning I may attach to it, then one will be arousing specific expectations among Black citizens if one tells them that one is promising them citizenship of the RSA and one grants it to them. I contend that it would not be remarkable if that were a factor contributing towards unrest and dissatisfaction among Blacks.</p>

<p>I want to go further. I want to know what is the meaning of the independence of the Republic of South Africa, the meaning of our independence, if the citizens of a neighbouring state may also be citizens of the Republic of South Africa, with or without a political say. If they have no political say, then the Government must say so. If they are going to have a political say then I want to know what is the meaning of the independence and the sovereignty of the Republic of South Africa, and, of course, of that of the Black states, unless it is not going to be a case of &#x201C;one Black, one vote&#x201D;, but rather &#x201C;one Black, two votes&#x201D;! Then it would mean that he would have a vote in an independent Black state and that he would be able to vote here as well. We want some clarity in that regard.</p>

<p>I also wish to ask whether it is far-fetched to argue that such a general South African citizenship could form the basis for an envisaged federation of South African states. I am not speaking about a confederation now, <span class="col_1763-1764" refersTo="page_0916"/>which is a loose association of independent states; I am speaking about a federation with a common citizenship. The separate states are, it is true, geographically separate to a degree, with a kind of second-level autonomy of their own, but there is an overall state machinery; an overall government.</p>

</speech>
